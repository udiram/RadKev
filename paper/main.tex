\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Categorical decisions in radiology}\label{sec:intro_decisions}

Many routine radiology workflows involve categorical decision making: whether a report describes a particular finding, which differential diagnosis within a specific subset of options is most likely, to which subspecialty a study should be assigned, and how urgently a result requires triage and follow-up. Increasing automation in such decisions requires a model that selects consistently and confidently among predefined answers and assigns a probability to its selection, so that highly confident decisions can be automated and uncertain ones can be flagged for human review \citep{geifman2017selective}. Large language models (LLMs) have been evaluated for many such decisions, including the extraction of findings from free-text reports \citep{fink2023datamining,mukherjee2023vicuna}, the detection of errors in reports \citep{gertz2024errors}, the selection of imaging according to the American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria \citep{rau2023accgpt,yao2025radcases}, diagnosis from the clinical history and imaging findings \citep{ueda2023diagnosisplease,tayebiarasteh2025radiorag} and radiology board-style questions \citep{bhayana2023board}. Three properties of LLMs limit their use for such decisions. First, an LLM returns generated text, which must be parsed and mapped onto the admissible answers and does not directly provide a probability for each answer. Second, text is generated sequentially, one token at a time, so that latency and compute increase with the length of the output, particularly when the model reasons before answering. Third, the most capable LLMs are often externally hosted and are therefore unsuitable for patient reports under institutional privacy constraints \citep{mukherjee2023vicuna}.

\subsection{Decision models}\label{sec:intro_dm}

Decision models, introduced by TypeSafe as so-called System One models, are designed for this setting \citep{almeida2026jev}. A decision model receives an input text, the state, together with typed questions (binary, multiple-choice or ordinal) whose admissible answers are specified in the request body, and returns a probability distribution over the admissible answers to each question. All questions are answered in a single query, in parallel, rather than by autoregressive generation \citep{almeida2026jev,typesafe2026api}, so every output is a valid answer by construction. Jev, released by TypeSafe in September 2026, is available only through an externally hosted interface, so that submitted data must leave the user's institution, and its architecture remains closed source \citep{typesafe2026api,hume2026unmasked}. The OpenAI Decisions API, released in public beta in October 2026, likewise provides a general-purpose decision model through an externally hosted interface \citep{openai2026decisions}.

Kev is an open-weight family of decision models, with 0.8 to 27 billion parameters, that implements the same TypeSafe-like interface on open-source Qwen backbones \citep{qwen38}, with an architecture inferred from Jev's external behavior \citep{palmer2026kev,hume2026unmasked}. Because its weights and training code are released under the Apache-2.0 license, Kev can be deployed within an institutional network and adapted to domain-specific data. These properties correspond closely to the requirements of radiology: the decisions are categorical and frequent, the several decisions required for one report can be answered in a single request, calibrated probabilities permit selective automation, and patient data need not leave the institution.

\subsection{Domain specialization}\label{sec:intro_spec}

In a benchmark evaluation, a general-purpose decision model, Jev, achieved accuracy comparable to that of the frontier LLM GPT-6 Sol with reasoning on questions derived from research abstracts, but lower accuracy on examination questions and substantially lower accuracy on diagnostic case challenges (59.8\% vs 82.4\% on DiagnosisArena), although its probabilities were better calibrated on examination questions \citep{madrid2026jevmed}. In radiology, Jev has been used to evaluate the factual consistency of generated reports \citep{huang2026jevrad}. For LLMs, the value of domain specialization is contested: domain-adaptive pretraining improves downstream performance \citep{gururangan2020dontstop}, whereas a generalist model with optimized prompting has exceeded medically fine-tuned models \citep{nori2023medprompt}. For decision models, domain adaptation has been reported for Chinese-language medical, legal and financial tasks \citep{wang2026chinesejev}. To our knowledge, no decision model has been specialized for radiology, and specialization has not been compared with the principal alternatives for drop-in use in radiology clinics: larger general-purpose decision models and LLMs of comparable size.

\subsection{Present study}\label{sec:intro_study}

In this study, we fine-tuned two general-purpose decision models, Kev-27B and Kev-9B, on \V{v3_train_records} records from public radiology sources to obtain RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B, and evaluated them on a benchmark of \V{v3_bench_q} held-out questions on decisions of the kinds for which LLMs have been evaluated in radiology, under an analysis plan specified before any result of these models was examined. We tested the hypothesis that a decision model specialized for radiology makes these decisions at least as accurately as general-purpose decision models and as an LLM of comparable size, at a fraction of the inference latency and compute of an LLM that generates its answers, because it answers every question about a record in a single forward pass without generating text. Specialization was compared with model scale, represented by the general-purpose decision models Kev-9B and Kev-27B, and with two LLMs of the same size, Qwen3.8-27B \citep{qwen38} and the medical model MedGemma-27B-text \citep{sellergren2025medgemma}, evaluated zero-shot with reasoning disabled on all questions and enabled on a sample of them. The specialized models were also compared with the OpenAI Decisions API and evaluated on an external test set of radiology reports from another institution \citep{delbrouck2024radgraphxl}. Because Kev-27B is built on Qwen3.8-27B, the design compares identical backbone weights used as an LLM, as a general-purpose decision model and as a radiology-specialized decision model. The weights of RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B, the training and evaluation code and the benchmark are publicly released.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:data}

The data used for this study are intended to cover three distinct decision-making modalities in radiology for which open-source data are available: reading a report, diagnosing or classifying a case and applying radiological knowledge, with human verified ground truth labels preferred wherever possible (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The report-reading and case tasks correspond to decisions made in radiology practice for which LLMs have been evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:intro}); the examination questions are not clinical tasks themselves and serve as a measure of the radiological knowledge on which these decisions rely. Each record pairs a state, i.e. the input text that the model ingests, with one or more typed questions whose admissible answers are given with the question: yes or no, a choice among named options, or an ordinal score. Report reading is represented by chest radiograph reports from IU/Open-i \citep{demnerfushman2016iu}, whose findings were indexed by human coders, posed as yes/no questions on whether a finding is present or the study is normal and as a four-option question on which finding the report describes; by chest CT reports from CT-RATE \citep{hamamci2026ctrate}, which were posed in the same way against the dataset's classifier labels for 18 abnormalities; and by ReXErr \citep{rao2025rexerr}, in which GPT-4o injected clinically meaningful errors into MIMIC-CXR reports \citep{johnson2019mimiccxr}: each record shows either the original or the error-injected version of a report, and the question asks whether the report contains an error, a key known by construction. Case diagnosis and classification are represented by teaching cases from Eurorad \citep{eurorad,wanglab2025eurorad}, in which the model reads the clinical history and imaging findings and selects the author's final diagnosis from the case's own differential diagnoses or the subspecialty section, among \V{n_sections}, under which the case was published, by RadCases \citep{yao2025radcases}, patient one-liners labeled by two senior medical students under an attending radiologist, with radiologist adjudication, with the ACR Appropriateness Criteria panel and topic that govern their imaging work-up, posed as a choice among \V{rc_panel_options} panel options (the panels and an option stating that no topic applies) and among \V{rc_topics} topics, and by RSNA-RadioQA \citep{tayebiarasteh2025radiorag}, case descriptions from the Case Collection of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), posed as a choice among four diagnoses. Radiological knowledge is represented by examination questions. To focus both training and evaluation on radiology, only radiology questions were taken from the examination collections: the radiology questions of MedMCQA \citep{pal2022medmcqa} and, from MedMCQA's other subjects, MedQA \citep{jin2021medqa}, the medical subjects of MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, PubMedQA \citep{jin2019pubmedqa} and MedXpertQA \citep{zuo2025medxpertqa}, the questions whose stem, state or options contain a term from a fixed list of imaging modalities and radiological terms (Supplementary Note~S2). The same selection was applied to all splits; it retained \V{rf_kept} of \V{rf_n} test questions and, for training, \V{v3r_medmcqa_train} of \V{v3r_medmcqa_train_all} MedMCQA and \V{v3r_medqa_train} of \V{v3r_medqa_train_all} MedQA records. Decisions about imaging orders, triage and follow-up otherwise have no public answer keys; questions of this kind were therefore generated from public reports and clinical indications and labeled by two LLMs (Section~\ref{sec:llmlabels}); these LLM-labeled questions were used only to augment the training data and not for testing. Records were divided into training, development and test splits by patient or case, with official test sets retained where available and one instruction wording per question kind withheld from training; IU, MMLU, PubMedQA and MedXpertQA were used only for development and testing. Of RadCases, only the openly available subsets were used, one-liners written by GPT-3.5 and the first sentences of Medbullets USMLE Step 2 and 3 questions \citep{chen2024medbullets}: \V{rc_matched} cases were matched to their labels, \V{rc_overlap} whose text occurred in another RadKev split were excluded, and the remainder were split by case into \V{rc_train} training, \V{rc_dev} development and \V{rc_test} test cases. ReXErr's official splits were used, with \V{rx_train} training and \V{rx_dev} development reports sampled and all \V{rx_test} test reports retained (\V{rx_test_err} with an injected error). RSNA-RadioQA was used only for testing: of its \V{rsna_published} published questions, the \V{rsna_q} whose text appears in the supplement of the original study were used, with the four answer options of the RaR study \citep{wind2025rar}, whose incorrect options were generated by LLMs and reviewed by a board-certified radiologist, and with the option matching the published reference answer as the key.

The benchmark thus measures performance on clinically relevant radiology decisions, together with the underlying radiological knowledge they require. It is restricted to radiology and comprises the \V{v3_bench_q} test questions of the tasks above: \V{v3_bench_human_q} whose keys were assigned by people (human-assigned questions) and the \V{rx_test} ReXErr questions, whose keys are known by construction. CT-RATE, whose keys come from a report classifier, is reported separately as agreement with the classifier, and the teacher-labeled questions are not benchmarked. Supplementary Note~S1 shows example records and the question formats of every source, and Supplementary Note~S2 gives the construction rules.

\subsection{LLM-labeled training data}\label{sec:llmlabels}

Decisions about imaging orders, triage and follow-up are routine in radiology, but no large-scale public datasets provide reliable human-generated answer keys for them, and labeling them by hand at the required scale was not feasible within this study. Questions on these decisions were therefore labeled by LLMs and used as a form of training-data augmentation: they extend the training data to decisions that have no public answer keys, transferring the judgments of two teacher models to the decision model as in knowledge distillation \citep{hinton2015distilling}, and they were not used for testing. Candidate questions on these decisions, of ten kinds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:teacher}), were generated from reports in CheXpert Plus \citep{chambon2024chexpertplus}, ReXGradient-160K \citep{zhang2025rexgradient} and CT-RATE and from clinical indications in these sources and in Eurorad. Two LLMs acted as teachers (Figure~\ref{fig:teacher}): MedGemma-27B-text \citep{sellergren2025medgemma}, a medical model, and Qwen3.8-27B \citep{qwen38}, a general-purpose model. Each read the state and the question with lettered options, with reasoning disabled, and its answer distribution was the softmax of its next-token logits over the option letters. In the run whose labels were used for training, the reasoning segment that MedGemma-27B-text opens by default was not closed, so its letter probabilities were read at the start of that segment rather than after it, which can penalize the model; the labels were later regenerated with the segment closed (Supplementary Note~S2). A question was retained only if both teachers ranked the same answer option first; the agreed-upon answer became the label, and the mean of the two distributions a soft training target \citep{hinton2015distilling}. Of \V{teacher_cand} candidates, \V{teacher_kept_first} (\V{teacher_kept_first_pct}\%) were retained for training. Machine-derived labels are widely used in radiology datasets \citep{irvin2019chexpert,hamamci2026ctrate}, LLM annotation has performed comparably to crowd-worker annotation on text-classification tasks \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt}, and agreement between independent labelers is an established filter for label errors \citep{brodley1999mislabeled}. Because LLM labels still require validation against human judgment \citep{pangakis2023validation}, and because keys produced by the LLM comparators would favor those models, the teacher-labeled questions were used only for training and are not part of the benchmark.

\subsection{Model and training}\label{sec:training}

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/fig_kev.pdf}
  \caption{Architecture of Kev, the decision model fine-tuned in this study, shown for Kev-27B. (a) A request consists of a state followed by one or more questions, each with its options and a decision token; every question attends to the state and to itself but not to the other questions, so the state is encoded once. (b) The backbone is Qwen3.8-27B: \V{bb_layers} layers in 16 repeating groups of three Gated DeltaNet (linear-attention) layers and one gated full-attention layer, with hidden size \V{bb_hidden}. Its weights are frozen, and a rank-\V{lora_rank} low-rank adapter (LoRA) is attached to every linear projection. (c) The pointer head projects the hidden states of the decision token and of each option's closing token to \V{head_dim} dimensions; the score of an option is the scaled dot product of the two projections, and the answer distribution is the softmax over the question's options after division by a temperature T. Kev-9B has the same structure on Qwen3.5-9B-Base.}
  \label{fig:kev}
\end{figure}

  \label{fig:training}

Kev \citep{palmer2026kev} adds a low-rank adapter (LoRA) \citep{hu2022lora} of rank \V{lora_rank} to every linear projection of a frozen language-model backbone, together with a pointer head \citep{vinyals2015pointer} (Figure~\ref{fig:kev}): the state is encoded once, each question attends to the state but not to the other questions, and the resultant answer distribution is a softmax over scores computed between the question and each of its options. RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B were obtained by continued training of the released Kev-27B and Kev-9B (revisions \V{rev_kev27} and \V{rev_kev9}), whose backbones are Qwen3.8-27B and Qwen3.5-9B-Base, so that the specialized models keep Kev's interface and differ from it only in their weights. The training split of all sources in Table~\ref{tab:data} (\V{v3_train_records} records) was interspersed with 1,000 records from Kev's own training data to limit the loss of general decision skill (of these \V{v3r_fed} records, \V{v3r_rejected} exceeded the training context of Kev's trainer, a state of 1,536 tokens, a question branch of 2,176 tokens or a packed record of 3,200 tokens, and were excluded at loading, leaving \V{v3r_accepted}), and Kev's prescribed augmentations were applied: options were reshuffled in each pass, and questions with a single correct answer were randomly modified with ``none of the above'' options or irrelevant distractors, whereas teacher-labeled questions were only reshuffled to preserve their soft targets. The loss was the cross-entropy with respect to the labeled answer or the soft target. Each model was trained for one epoch (\V{v3r_steps} optimizer steps) with AdamW and a one-cycle schedule, at a peak learning rate of $3\times10^{-5}$ for RadKev-27B and $2\times10^{-5}$ for RadKev-9B and an effective batch size of 8 records, with bfloat16 computation on four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs; the frozen weights were held in \V{v3_wdtype_27b} precision for RadKev-27B and in \V{v3_wdtype_9b} precision for RadKev-9B. Both models were trained with data-parallel processes whose adapter and pointer-head gradients were summed before each optimizer step, which, because the loss is normalized by the number of records in the step, is equivalent to training in a single process: RadKev-27B with two processes, each holding the frozen backbone split across one NVLink-connected pair of GPUs and processing micro-batches of 2 records with \V{v3_accum_27b} accumulation steps (\V{v3_hours_27b} hours), and RadKev-9B with four single-GPU processes and micro-batches of 2 records without accumulation (\V{v3_hours_9b} hours). A temperature was then fitted for each model on the development split by minimizing the negative log-likelihood \citep{guo2017calibration} (\V{v3_T_27b} for RadKev-27B and \V{v3_T_9b} for RadKev-9B). Supplementary Note~S3 gives the model revisions, the remaining hyperparameters and the software versions.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

The models, the benchmark and the outcomes were specified in an analysis plan written before any result of these models was read. The benchmark comprises 15 tasks: three IU/Open-i tasks (finding present, normal study and which finding), Eurorad diagnosis and subspecialty classification, the RadCases panel and topic questions, RSNA-RadioQA, ReXErr error detection, and one task for each examination source (the MedMCQA radiology subject, the other MedMCQA subjects, MedQA, MedXpertQA, MMLU and PubMedQA), in which that source's radiology questions were pooled because the radiology selection left some subjects with very few questions. The primary outcome was task-averaged accuracy, the unweighted mean of the accuracies of the 15 tasks, compared between RadKev-27B and Kev-27B. Secondary outcomes were the same mean over the 14 human-assigned tasks; accuracy over all benchmark questions and on every task; the differences between RadKev-27B and each LLM; expected calibration error and Brier score; the rate of confident errors; and coverage at a 5\% error budget \citep{geifman2017selective}. Every decision model was scored on the same test records, which contain the \V{v3_bench_q} benchmark questions, and received each record once, with all of its questions in a single request. Released models that are not pinned to a revision were loaded from fixed local snapshots, whose hashes were recorded. The comparators separate the effects of specialization, scale and model type: Kev-27B and Kev-9B \citep{palmer2026kev}, the starting points of RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B, measure specialization, and their difference measures scale within the same family; Qwen3.8-27B, the backbone of Kev-27B and RadKev-27B, measures the same network queried directly as an LLM; MedGemma-27B-text is an LLM specialized for medicine; and the OpenAI Decisions API, an externally hosted general-purpose decision model, represents the hosted alternative (Section~\ref{sec:additional}). The LLMs were scored with the teacher prompt, one question per prompt, from the softmax of the next-token logits over the option letters with reasoning disabled, on every benchmark question with at most 16 options, which excludes the RadCases topic question (Section~\ref{sec:res_answerspace}), and every comparison with an LLM used the questions answered by both systems. Post hoc, the LLMs were also scored with reasoning enabled on a sample of \V{r3_rs_q} benchmark questions (Supplementary Note~S4). Because the teacher-labeled questions, which the LLMs had produced, are not benchmarked, the LLMs are compared with the decision models on every benchmark task they could answer.

The catch-all option of the RadCases panel question, stating that no ACR Appropriateness Criteria topic applies, was the most frequent panel answer in the training split (\V{r3_rc_train_none} of \V{r3_rc_train_q} questions, \V{r3_rc_train_none_pct}\%), and the specialized models reproduced this frequency as a prior and selected the option by default (Section~\ref{sec:res_primary}). Their answer distributions on this question were therefore corrected post hoc for the training answer frequencies \citep{saerens2002adjusting}: the probability of each of the 12 options was divided by that option's relative frequency among the training-split panel answers, estimated with add-one smoothing, and the distribution was renormalized, which corresponds to assuming that every option is equally frequent at test time. The correction uses only training labels and involves no retraining. It was applied to RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B, which were trained on RadCases, and not to the other systems, which were not, and all results reported for RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B include it.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}

All comparisons were paired: both systems were evaluated on identical questions, and differences are reported as the first system minus the second. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were obtained by a cluster bootstrap \citep{efron1993bootstrap} with \V{n_boot} resamples, in which test records were resampled within each source together with all of their questions, so that questions about the same report or case were never separated. One set of resamples was shared by all systems, so that every difference, and every difference between two differences, was computed on the same resamples. Intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled statistic; for task-averaged accuracy, the statistic is the mean of the per-task differences, and differences in calibration, confident errors and coverage were estimated on the first \V{n_boot_cal} resamples. Intervals for Kev's transfer suite, which consists of different questions, were obtained with 1,000 resamples. No formal sample-size calculation was performed; the test set comprised all eligible held-out records of each source (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Two-sided $p$ values were derived from the bootstrap distribution and adjusted by the Holm procedure \citep{holm1979simple} across the 15 tasks for the primary comparison, as specified in the addendum, and within each other set of comparisons reported together, for which the adjustment was not prespecified.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Additional analyses}\label{sec:additional}

The following analyses were specified in the addendum or added later and are reported as post hoc; none involved training. The effect of specialization was compared with that of scale as a difference of differences, the gain from specialization (RadKev-27B minus Kev-27B, or RadKev-9B minus Kev-9B) minus the gain from scale (Kev-27B minus Kev-9B), computed on identical resamples. To test whether specialization reduced general decision performance, each specialized model and its starting checkpoint were compared on Kev's out-of-domain transfer suite (\V{transfer_n} questions). The gain of each specialized model was compared between withheld and seen instruction wordings; and calibration was re-estimated after recalibrating every system on the test split by two-fold cross-fitted temperature scaling. An options-only control tested whether the models need the case to answer case-based questions. Every Eurorad diagnosis question of the test split and every RSNA-RadioQA question was posed again with the case removed: the state, which contains the patient's age, sex, clinical history and imaging findings (Eurorad) or the case description (RSNA-RadioQA), was replaced by the single sentence ``No case information is available.'', while the instructions and the answer options were left unchanged. A model that relies on the case should then fall to the accuracy expected by chance, one divided by the number of options; accuracy that remains well above chance indicates that the correct option can be recognized from the options themselves. RadKev-27B, Kev-27B, RadKev-9B, Kev-9B and the two LLMs were scored with and without the case, and the gains from specialization were compared between the two conditions. In addition, Eurorad diagnosis questions were divided according to whether a distinctive word of the correct option, at least six letters long and absent from the other options, appears in the case text. Because a study is routed to a subspecialty reading list before its images are interpreted, the Eurorad subspecialty question was also posed with the imaging findings removed from its state and age, sex and clinical history kept (pre-read condition). The effect of the answer space was examined on \V{as3_n_dx} Eurorad diagnosis questions of the test split and \V{as3_n_rsna} RSNA-RadioQA questions by holding the case and the correct answer fixed and replacing the other options with randomly chosen or semantically similar diagnoses, for answer spaces of \V{as3_sizes} options, or with plausible incorrect answers generated by Qwen3.8-27B, for up to 16 options (Supplementary Note~S5). Because single-letter scoring is limited by the number of letters, the LLMs were scored on these answer spaces by greedy generation of the number of the chosen option, at every size, and additionally by option-letter probabilities up to 16 options; accuracy and latency were measured as a function of the number of options for every system. Latency, the measure of inference cost, was measured on \V{lat3_records} records drawn at random from the benchmark, on identical hardware, one request at a time, on one NVLink pair of RTX A6000 GPUs with Hugging Face Transformers in bfloat16; a decision model answered all questions of a record in one pass, whereas an LLM was called once per question, with option-letter scoring, with direct generation of the answer and, for Qwen3.8-27B on \V{lat3_reason_q} questions, with reasoning.

The OpenAI Decisions API \citep{openai2026decisions} (model gpt-6-luna, which at the time of writing is the only model available through the API), an externally hosted general-purpose decision model released in public beta during the study, was scored on the same benchmark questions. Each benchmark record was sent once, with all of its benchmark questions in a single request (yes/no questions as predicates, multiple-choice questions as choices and ordinal questions as scores), with the instructions and options that every other system received and without additional prompting; CT-RATE questions were not sent. The returned probability of each admissible answer was scored as for the other systems; because the API returns probabilities rounded to two decimal places, it was not recalibrated. Questions that the API rejected (\V{r3_od_refusals}) received a uniform distribution. Latency was measured end to end, from the institution's network to the response, for \V{r3_lt_od_seq_n} requests sent one at a time and for the remaining \V{r3_lt_od_n} requests sent with four in flight, and the processing time reported by the API was recorded. The entire benchmarking run cost US\$\V{r3_od_cost}, showing the cost effectiveness of these decision models, but also highlighting the need for a safe, locally hosted, open-source option.

As an external test on data from another institution that no model was trained on, RadGraph-XL \citep{delbrouck2024radgraphxl} provides \V{rg_reports} reports annotated by radiologists, \V{rg_per_mod} each of chest radiographs, chest CT, abdominal and pelvic CT and brain MRI. Reports that overlapped with a CheXpert Plus report or a RadKev record (a word 8-gram overlap of 50\% or more) were excluded (\V{rg_excluded}, nearly all chest radiograph reports), leaving \V{rg_records} reports. From each, up to three annotated observations were sampled, at most one per status, and posed as the four-option status question of the training format (present, explicitly absent, possible or equivocal, not mentioned), with the radiologists' annotation as the key (\V{rg_q} questions). The present models, their starting points and the two LLMs were scored on this test set in the same way as on the benchmark.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S2. Record construction}

\paragraph{IU/Open-i.} Medical Subject Headings assigned by the indexers were mapped to \V{iu_vocab} findings. A finding was considered present if one of its terms had been assigned and absent otherwise, and a study was considered normal if it had been indexed as normal. Reports with fewer than 20 characters of findings and impression, and reports without indexing, were excluded. Each report received two presence questions, chosen so that one finding was present and one absent where possible, one normal-study question and, for half of the reports with at least one finding, a question on which of four findings (one present, three absent) was described. The license (CC BY-NC-ND) does not permit derivative works, so the collection was used only for development (25\% of reports, by a hash of the report identifier) and testing (75\%).

\paragraph{CT-RATE.} The collection comprises 25,692 chest CT studies from 21,304 patients \citep{hamamci2026ctrate}; its labels were produced by a RadBERT classifier fine-tuned on manually annotated reports. One report per study received three presence questions and the normal-study and which-finding questions; a study was normal if none of the 18 abnormalities was labeled. The official training set was divided by patient into training and development records (8\% development), the official validation set served as the test set, and training used a random sample of \V{ctrate_cap} reports.

\paragraph{Eurorad.} The state contained age, sex, clinical history and imaging findings, from which sentences mentioning pathology, biopsy, histology, surgery, treatment, follow-up, confirmation or diagnosis were removed by keyword matching. Cases whose final diagnosis did not appear among the differential diagnoses, that had fewer than two differential diagnoses or whose remaining findings were shorter than 40 characters were excluded, as were development and test cases whose remaining text contained the diagnosis. Half of the cases received the subspecialty question, whose key was the Eurorad section of the case (the first listed when several were given) and whose options were shown with a one-line description of the section in 60\% of cases. Because the state contained the imaging findings, this question classifies an interpreted case; it does not reproduce the routing of a study before interpretation, which relies on the order, the indication and the examination type. Age and sex were taken from the case metadata and occasionally differ from those stated in the history, as in the example of Supplementary Note~S1. Cases were split 80\%, 10\% and 10\% by a hash of the case identifier.

\paragraph{RSNA-RadioQA.} The case descriptions and reference answers were taken from Appendix~S1 of the original study \citep{tayebiarasteh2025radiorag}, which omits question 44 of the \V{rsna_published}, and the four answer options of each question from the public RadSaFE-200 release (RadioRAG subset), which contains the options of the RaR study \citep{wind2025rar}. The state was the case description and the question its final sentence, which asks for the most likely diagnosis; options were presented under neutral keys in a fixed random order. The key was the option matching the reference answer of the appendix, which every question contained; for \V{rsna_keyfix} questions, the answer index of the option release designates a different option, and the reference answer was used.

\paragraph{RadCases.} The RadCases release (Hugging Face \texttt{michaelsyao/RadCases}, revision 93c99559) labels each patient summary, a one-sentence description of the presentation, with the ACR Appropriateness Criteria panel and topic that apply to it; the labels were assigned by two senior medical students under the supervision of an attending radiologist, with disagreements resolved by radiologists \citep{yao2025radcases}. The release contains only SHA-512 hashes of the summaries. Their text was rebuilt from the two openly available subsets, summaries written by GPT-3.5 for the original study and the first sentences of Medbullets USMLE Step 2 and 3 questions \citep{chen2024medbullets}, with the sentence splitter of the original study, and a case was retained only if the hash of its rebuilt text matched a label (\V{rc_matched} of \V{rc_label_rows}). Summaries whose text occurs in any other record of this study (\V{rc_overlap}) were excluded, and the remaining cases were split by a hash of the summary into training (60\%), development (10\%) and test (30\%) cases. Each case received the panel question unless it was labeled with more than one panel (\V{rc_multi_panel} cases) and the topic question if it was labeled with exactly one known topic. The panel question offered the 11 panels and the option ``None: no ACR Appropriateness Criteria topic applies'', the topic question all \V{rc_topics} topics of the criteria version used by the original study, both under neutral, shuffled keys. The state was the summary alone.

\paragraph{ReXErr.} ReXErr-v1 \citep{rao2025rexerr} pairs MIMIC-CXR reports \citep{johnson2019mimiccxr} with versions into which GPT-4o inserted clinically meaningful errors, sampled across error types that occur in reports written by radiologists and by generative models. At the report level, each study was represented by one record whose state was either the original or the error-injected report, chosen by a hash of the study identifier so that about half of the records contain an error, and whose single question asked whether the report contains an error; the key is therefore known by construction. Studies whose two versions were identical or empty were excluded. The official splits were used, with \V{rx_train} training and \V{rx_dev} development reports sampled at random and all \V{rx_test} test reports retained (\V{rx_test_err} with an error).

\paragraph{Examination questions.} MedMCQA: all radiology questions were retained; other subjects were sampled at approximately 20\% and capped at \V{medmcqa_cap} training questions per subject; questions with empty or duplicate options were removed; because the official test answers are not public, the official validation set served as the test set, and 5\% of the official training set was held out for development. MedQA: the official test set was retained and 10\% of the official training set was held out for development. MMLU: the anatomy, clinical knowledge, college biology, college medicine, medical genetics and professional medicine subjects, with the official validation and test sets used for development and testing. PubMedQA: the expert-annotated subset, divided equally between development and testing. MedXpertQA: the text subset, 20\% for development and 80\% for testing. For the present models, every split of these collections was restricted to radiology: all MedMCQA radiology questions were kept, and a question from any other subject or collection was kept if its stem, state or options contained a term from a fixed list of imaging modalities and radiological terms (for example radiograph, X-ray, computed tomography, magnetic resonance, ultrasound, mammography, scintigraphy, angiography, fluoroscopy, contrast agent, imaging, radiology and radiopaque), with acronyms such as CT, MRI, PET and SPECT matched only in capitals. The list was fixed before any result of the present models was read.

\paragraph{Teacher-labeled questions.} Up to \V{teacher_per_source} reports and \V{teacher_per_source} indications were drawn per source, including Eurorad case presentations for indications. Five kinds concern a report (critical finding, urgency, follow-up, interval change and incidental finding), one the status of a finding in a chest radiograph report, and four an imaging order (first examination, use of contrast, priority and appropriateness of an ordered examination); order questions were posed on the clinical indication alone. Each teacher was instructed to answer as an attending radiologist, with reasoning disabled and the options as a lettered list. Because MedGemma's chat template does not suppress its reasoning segment, a one-sentence segment (``I will answer with the letter only.'') was pre-filled so that the next token is the answer. In the run whose labels were used for training, agreement ranged from \V{agree_first_min}\% (use of contrast) to \V{agree_first_max}\% (finding status). The labels used for training come from a first run in which MedGemma was scored without the pre-filled segment (\V{teacher_kept_first} questions retained, \V{teacher_kept_first_pct}\%). All candidates were later rescored by MedGemma with the pre-filled segment and the labels regenerated, with the answers of Qwen3.8-27B unchanged. Of the \V{teacher_train_first} training questions retained in the first run, \V{teacher_train_same} (\V{teacher_train_same_pct}\%) were also retained in the regenerated run, and no question retained in both runs received a different label. RadKev-27B and RadKev-9B were trained with the labels of the first run.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S3. Training details}

\paragraph{Augmentation.} Each multiple-choice question with a single correct answer received at most one modification: the correct option was replaced by a ``none of the above'' option, which then became the correct answer (probability \V{p_none}; questions with more than two options); a ``none of the above'' option was added as a wrong alternative (probability \V{p_none_distract}); or an irrelevant distractor was added (probability \V{p_distract}). With probability \V{p_none_pair}, a record additionally contributed a minimal pair in which the same question was posed with a ``none of the above'' option, once with and once without its correct option. With the \V{replay} replayed Kev records, training comprised \V{v3r_accepted} records and \V{v3_examples_27b} examples for RadKev-27B (\V{v3_examples_9b} for RadKev-9B), including the minimal pairs.

\paragraph{Optimization.} AdamW with weight decay \V{weight_decay}; one-cycle schedule with 10\% warm-up and cosine decay; gradient-norm clipping at 1.0; 8 records per optimizer step, from micro-batches of 2 records across data-parallel processes and accumulation steps; gradient checkpointing; states limited to \V{max_state} tokens, question branches to 2,176 tokens and packed records to 3,200 tokens, limits that \V{v3r_rejected} training records exceeded and that excluded them from training; random seed 0. The temperature was selected from \V{grid_points} logarithmically spaced values between 0.25 and 4.

\paragraph{Models.} Kev-27B, Hugging Face revision \V{rev_kev27}, on Qwen3.8-27B revision \V{rev_qwen27}; Kev-9B, revision \V{rev_kev9}, on Qwen3.5-9B-Base revision \V{rev_qwen9}. The pointer head projects the hidden states of each option's closing token and of the question's decision token into a \V{head_dim}-dimensional space, and the logit of an option is the scaled dot product of the two projections.

\paragraph{Hardware and software.} Peak memory, recorded on the first GPU of the first process, was \V{v3_peak_27b} GiB for RadKev-27B and \V{v3_peak_9b} GiB for RadKev-9B. For Kev-9B, splitting the model by layers across two GPUs reproduced single-GPU probabilities exactly on \V{parity_q} development questions. Software: Kev at commit \V{kev_commit} with two local patches, released with the code, that split a model across a pair of GPUs and add data-parallel training of the adapter (summation of gradients across processes, saving by the first process and a periodic check that all processes hold identical weights, which passed at each of the \V{v3_dpchecks_rk27} checks of both runs), PyTorch \V{ver_torch} with CUDA \V{ver_cuda}, Transformers \V{ver_transformers}, PEFT \V{ver_peft}, flash-linear-attention \V{ver_fla} and causal-conv1d \V{ver_cc1d}.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S4. Evaluation details}

\paragraph{Decision models.} Each record was submitted once, with all of its questions, in bfloat16; each model used its own temperature (the released temperature for the general-purpose models and the fitted temperature for RadKev). The answer to a question is the option with the highest probability, and that probability is its confidence. Every model accepted every test question.

\paragraph{Language models.} The prompt was that used for teacher labeling: the state, the question and its options as a lettered list, followed by an instruction to answer with one letter. MedGemma-27B-text was scored with the one-sentence pre-filled reasoning segment described in Supplementary Note~S2. With reasoning enabled, Qwen3.8-27B was run in its thinking mode, and for MedGemma-27B-text the prompt opened its reasoning segment so that the model always reasoned, using vLLM \V{ver_vllm} in bfloat16, each model's default sampling settings, seed 0 and at most \V{reason_think_cap} reasoning tokens. After the reasoning, ``Answer:'' was appended, and the answer distribution was read from the next-token probabilities of the option letters. When the model had written an answer after its reasoning (the first option letter in the generated text) and that answer differed from the letter read-out, the written answer was taken, with a probability of 0.98; this applied to none of the questions for Qwen3.8-27B and to \V{ovr_mg_pct}\% for MedGemma-27B-text. The reasoning sample comprised \V{r3_rs_q} benchmark questions, selected in a fixed hash order that does not depend on any model output: every Eurorad and MedMCQA radiology question, up to \V{reason_iu_cap} questions from each IU task and \V{reason_cap} from each other human-assigned task, every RSNA-RadioQA question, up to 150 RadCases panel questions and 200 ReXErr questions.

\paragraph{Outcomes.} The expected calibration error was computed over ten equal-width bins of confidence. A confident error is a question answered incorrectly with a confidence of at least 0.9. Coverage at a 5\% error budget is the largest proportion of questions that can be accepted in descending order of confidence while the error among the accepted questions does not exceed 5\%.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S5. Answer-space analysis}

For each question, the state, the instructions and the human-assigned key were held fixed, and only the other options were varied. The questions were \V{as3_n_dx} Eurorad diagnosis questions of the test split and \V{as3_n_rsna} RSNA-RadioQA questions, the first in a fixed hash order of their identifiers. Pool distractors were drawn from all distinct Eurorad diagnosis options of the training, development and test splits, and for RSNA-RadioQA additionally from its own options, either at random or in order of decreasing similarity to the key, measured as the cosine between PubMedBERT sentence embeddings \citep{gu2021pubmedbert}. LLM distractors were generated by Qwen3.8-27B, which received the state, the question and the correct answer and was asked, with reasoning disabled and greedy decoding, for 15 plausible but incorrect answers; the key was never taken from the LLM. A candidate distractor was discarded if its normalized text contained, or was contained in, that of the key or of an already selected distractor, or if its embedding cosine to either was at least 0.95. Each question was posed with its original options; with the key and random or most similar pool distractors, for \V{as3_sizes} options in total; with the key and LLM distractors, for \V{as3_llm_sizes} options; and with the original options supplemented with up to 8 LLM distractors. An answer space was omitted for a question when too few distractors remained after filtering. Each answer space was posed as a separate question on the same state. RadKev-27B, Kev-27B, RadKev-9B and Kev-9B scored every option directly. The LLMs received the options as a numbered list and generated the number of their answer by greedy decoding, with reasoning disabled; an output that was not a number in range was counted as incorrect, and its rate is reported. Up to 16 options, the LLMs were additionally scored by option-letter probabilities, as in the main evaluation. Accuracy was estimated with 95\% intervals from a bootstrap over questions. Latency as a function of the number of options was measured for every system with one question per request on \V{as3_lat_cases} Eurorad cases with \V{as3_lat_sizes} options, one request at a time, with the first \V{as3_lat_warmup} requests excluded; the LLMs were timed with numbered generation at every size and with option-letter scoring at up to 16 options. In the main latency measurement, the first \V{lat_warmup} requests of each mode (the first request for the LLM with reasoning) were excluded, and the per-question latency of a decision model was its latency per record divided by the number of questions.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note S6. External test set}

\paragraph{RadGraph-XL.} The Stanford portion of RadGraph-XL comprises \V{ext_rg_reports} reports, 500 each of chest radiographs, chest CT, abdominal and pelvic CT and brain MRI, in which board-certified radiologists annotated every entity and its status \citep{delbrouck2024radgraphxl}. Reports sharing at least half of their word 8-grams with a CheXpert Plus report or with any record of this study were excluded (\V{ext_rg_excl} reports, all of them chest radiograph reports, which derive from the same institution as CheXpert Plus), so that the test consists almost entirely of CT and MRI reports. For each remaining report, one observation annotated as definitely present, one as definitely absent and one as uncertain were drawn at random where available, excluding spans that consist only of a generic qualifier such as ``normal'' or ``stable'', and each was posed in the format of the finding-status questions used in training, with the annotated span as the finding and the options reported as present, explicitly reported as absent, possible or equivocal, and not mentioned in the report; the last was never correct. The state was the full report text.
