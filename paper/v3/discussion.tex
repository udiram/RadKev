\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Fine-tuning a general-purpose decision model on public radiology data improved the prespecified primary outcome on a benchmark restricted to radiology. The hypothesis that the specialized model makes these decisions at least as accurately as general-purpose decision models and as LLMs of the same size was supported for Kev-27B, MedGemma-27B-text and Qwen3.8-27B, over the benchmark as a whole and over the human-assigned tasks alone. % [C1] [C4] [C5] [C16]
Three findings qualify this result. First, the effect of specialization differed between tasks: it was largest in the detection of errors in reports and in Eurorad diagnosis and absent in finding presence and subspecialty classification, and in the selection of the ACR Appropriateness Criteria panel, the specialized models adopted the catch-all option that no topic applies, the most frequent answer in the training split, as their default and matched their starting point only after a correction for the training answer frequencies. % [C3]
Second, before specialization the backbone was about as accurate when queried as a general-purpose decision model as when queried as an LLM, so the advantage of the specialized model over the LLM arose from specialization. % [C6] [C16]
Third, averaged over tasks, the gain from specialization at 27B did not differ detectably from that from a threefold increase in model size, and pooled over the human-assigned questions, which are dominated by report reading, it was larger. % [C8]
The secondary and additional analyses are exploratory.

These results extend earlier observations on domain adaptation. For LLMs, continued training on domain text improves downstream performance \citep{gururangan2020dontstop}, whereas prompting alone has allowed a generalist model to exceed medically fine-tuned models \citep{nori2023medprompt}; for clinical and radiology text, lightweight adaptation of open models has produced summaries comparable or superior to those of medical experts \citep{vanveen2024adapted} and is regarded as an alternative to general-purpose models for radiology reports \citep{huisman2025lightweight}, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning of locally deployed open models is recommended where prompting and retrieval are insufficient \citep{bluethgen2025bestpractices,savage2025opensource}. For decision models, an evaluation of Jev found accuracy comparable to that of a frontier LLM on questions about research abstracts but lower accuracy on examination questions and diagnostic cases \citep{madrid2026jevmed}, and domain adaptation has been reported for Chinese-language medical, legal and financial decisions \citep{wang2026chinesejev}. The present results indicate that, for the radiology decisions studied, a specialized open decision model can exceed a medically trained LLM of the same size and its own backbone used as an LLM. % [C5] [C16]
LLMs have been evaluated for the detection of errors in radiology reports \citep{gertz2024errors} and for the selection of imaging under the ACR Appropriateness Criteria \citep{rau2023accgpt,yao2025radcases}; in the present study, specialization on several thousand labeled reports improved error detection substantially, whereas on panel selection the composition of the training answers induced a default to the catch-all option that had to be corrected. % [C3]
Because no LLM was fine-tuned on the same data, the study does not separate the effect of the decision format from that of domain fine-tuning as such.

Compared with LLMs, the specialized model offers a different balance of accuracy, cost and calibration. A decision model answers all questions about a report in one pass and returns a probability for every admissible answer, which permits automation of confident decisions and referral of uncertain ones \citep{geifman2017selective}: at a 5\% error budget, RadKev-27B could answer \V{r3_cal_v3_27_cov}\% of the human-assigned questions, compared with \V{r3_cal_stock27_cov}\% for Kev-27B and \V{r3_cal_qwen38_cov}\% for Qwen3.8-27B. % [C11]
Its expected calibration error was similar to that of the model it was specialized from, but it made more confident errors (\V{r3_cal_v3_27_ce}\% vs \V{r3_cal_stock27_ce}\% of questions); thresholds for automation should therefore be set, and the temperature refitted, on local data before its probabilities are used \citep{guo2017calibration}. % [C12] [C15]
Per question, an LLM scored from its option-letter logits was about as fast as the decision model (\V{r3_lt_q} vs \V{r3_lt_rk27} ms), and with 255 options an LLM generating the number of its choice was faster (\V{r3_as_lat_q_255} vs \V{r3_as_lat_rk27_255} ms); the advantage in latency applies to LLMs that generate their answers with reasoning, and to records with several questions, which a decision model answers in one pass. % [C21] [C19]

Two analyses bear on what the benchmark measures. Removing the imaging findings from the subspecialty question reduced the accuracy of every system substantially, so classification of an interpreted case, the task as posed in the benchmark, is easier than routing before the read, and accuracies on the full state should not be taken as estimates of routing performance. % [C13]
For RadKev-27B, the gain was smaller on instruction wordings withheld from training than on those seen in training (\V{r3_wd_held} vs \V{r3_wd_seen} points), so part of its gain depended on familiar question phrasing; for RadKev-9B, the gain did not differ between the two (\V{r3_wd9_held} vs \V{r3_wd9_seen} points). % [C17]
The options-only control qualifies the gain on Eurorad diagnosis: without the case, the specialized models still selected the correct diagnosis in most questions, and their gain over the general-purpose models was larger than with the case, so this gain reflects recognition of the case author's final diagnosis from the wording of the options rather than reading of the case. The same caution applies to other benchmarks whose incorrect options are written by the authors of the correct answer. % [C18]

\subsection{Clinical implementation}\label{sec:disc_clinical}

\paragraph{Suitable decisions.} The considerations in this subsection follow from the present results and from the design of decision models; none of the uses described was evaluated in this study. Of the roles proposed for language models in radiology, which also include report drafting, summarization and patient communication \citep{paschali2025foundation,bluethgen2025bestpractices}, a decision model addresses the categorical decisions and complements, rather than replaces, generative systems. The approach appears suited to frequent, categorical decisions made on radiology text for which the admissible answers can be listed in advance: examination selection from an order's clinical indication, prioritization of requests and worklists, routing of studies to subspecialty readers, detection of critical or incidental findings and of errors in reports, extraction of follow-up recommendations for tracking, and consistency checks between a report's findings and its impression. A decision model answers all questions about a report in one pass and returns a probability for each admissible answer, so a single request can populate several fields of a worklist or a quality dashboard, and confident decisions can be automated while uncertain ones are referred for review. It is less suited to decisions whose answers cannot be listed in advance, to the generation of text such as report impressions, and to decisions that depend on the images themselves.

\paragraph{Resources and hardware.} All components are openly available and can be run on institutional hardware, so patient data need not leave the institution; the RadKev weights are licensed for non-commercial research use only. In this study, inference with the 27B models used two GPUs with 48 GB of memory each and with the 9B models one; specialization required \V{r3_hours_27} h of training for RadKev-27B and \V{r3_hours_9} h for RadKev-9B, each on four such GPUs, so retraining on an institution's own data would be feasible on a single workstation. The 9B model offers a lower hardware requirement at a moderate loss of accuracy (benchmark task mean \V{r3_a_v3_9_tm}\% vs \V{r3_a_v3_27_tm}\%). Beyond hardware, deployment would require integration with the radiology information system to supply report text and receive decisions, a labeled sample of local cases on which to measure accuracy and refit the temperature, radiologist time to define and review the answer spaces, and monitoring of accuracy and calibration over time.

\paragraph{Latency and throughput.} RadKev-27B answered a question in a median of \V{r3_lt_rk27} ms and RadKev-9B in \V{r3_lt_rk9} ms, one request at a time, compared with \V{r3_lt_qthink} s for an LLM with reasoning. An LLM scored only from its option-letter logits was about as fast per question (\V{r3_lt_q} ms), but this form of use returns one answer per request and is limited to answer spaces that single letters can label; with more options, the LLM must generate the number of its choice. At these latencies, one GPU pair answers about \V{r3_thr_rk27_hr} questions per hour without batching (\V{r3_thr_rk9_hr} for the 9B model on one GPU), estimates that are extrapolated from the measured latencies, assume uninterrupted sequential requests and were not measured in a clinical system. Because every option is encoded as part of its question, latency increases with the number of options (Section~\ref{sec:res_answerspace}), which favors short answer spaces.

\paragraph{Choosing the answer space.} A decision model can only choose among the answers it is offered, so the answer space is part of every decision, and its composition affected accuracy more than its size (Section~\ref{sec:res_answerspace}). An answer space that omits the correct answer forces an error, and one filled with implausible alternatives overstates accuracy. For decisions with a closed set of answers, such as the examination to order, the protocol, the priority or the follow-up category, the answer space can be taken from the institution's own order catalog, protocol list, triage scale or structured-reporting template; such lists are defined once, reviewed by radiologists, versioned and audited. For open-ended decisions such as differential diagnosis, a candidate list can be generated for each case and the decision model used to choose among the candidates and to attach a calibrated probability to the choice: candidates may be retrieved from a curated catalog of diagnoses by similarity to the case text, restricted by modality, body region, age and sex, or written by an LLM asked for a differential diagnosis. Lists of tens rather than hundreds of options keep latency low. A catch-all option, such as ``none of the above'' or ``no topic applies'', provides a means of indicating that the correct answer is missing, but its share of the training answers determines how often a fine-tuned model selects it: on the RadCases panel question, where it was the most frequent training answer, the specialized models selected it for most questions to which a panel applied until their answer distributions were corrected for the training answer frequencies (Section~\ref{sec:res_primary}). The answer spaces used for training should therefore be curated together with those used at deployment, including the frequency of catch-all options relative to the specific answers. % [C3]

\paragraph{Safeguards.} Whatever the method of building answer spaces, its inclusion rate, the proportion of locally labeled cases in which the correct answer appears among the candidates, should be measured before deployment, because a decision model cannot recover an answer it is not offered. The temperature should be refitted on local cases and on the answer spaces that will be used, because calibration depends on both, and thresholds for automation should be set on that local sample rather than taken from this study. Decisions with clinical consequences should remain subject to review by a radiologist, as the model's developers also recommend \citep{palmer2026kev}, and accuracy and calibration should be monitored after deployment, because case mix, report style and order catalogs change over time.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The RadCases and ReXErr test splits come from the same collections as their training splits, so the results on them are in-distribution; ReXErr's errors were inserted by an LLM, and its keys are known by construction rather than assigned by people. The only test set from a source not used in training was RadGraph-XL (Section~\ref{sec:res_external}). RSNA-RadioQA comprises \V{r3_n_rsna} questions, and several knowledge tasks retain few questions after the radiology filter, so their per-task estimates are imprecise; the filter is a fixed list of imaging terms and may include questions in which imaging is incidental. Most human-assigned questions come from a single collection of chest radiograph reports (IU/Open-i), which dominates any average over questions. The questions on imaging orders, triage and follow-up were labeled by agreement of two LLMs without validation against human judgment \citep{pangakis2023validation} and were used for training only. The subspecialty question was posed with the imaging findings, which are not available when a study is assigned to a reading worklist, and its key was the section under which the teaching case was published; it measures the classification of an interpreted case rather than routing, and with the findings removed, accuracy fell by \V{r3_pr_minchg} to \V{r3_pr_maxchg} percentage points (Section~\ref{sec:res_robust}). % [C13]
 All data were drawn from public datasets and teaching collections, the models were not validated on institutional reports, and accuracy was not examined across patient subgroups or institutions. The LLMs were evaluated zero-shot with a single prompt, and no LLM was fine-tuned on the same data. MedGemma-27B-text was evaluated without reasoning in the main evaluation, so its results may underestimate the capability of the model. Every model was trained once, with a single random seed. Public examination questions and Eurorad cases may have been part of the pretraining data of every model, so paired differences, rather than absolute accuracies, are the basis of the conclusions. The benchmark and outcomes were specified in an addendum to the analysis plan before any result of the present models was read; the analysis plan was not publicly registered. Latency was measured one request at a time; LLMs served with batching may achieve higher throughput. Finally, all questions were posed on text, and the study does not address decisions that require the images themselves.

\subsection{Future work}\label{sec:future}

Several questions remain open. The specialized model should be compared with radiologists in a reader study on institutional reports, in which the answer spaces are defined by the institution's own catalogs, and its error-detection and appropriateness decisions should be tested on reports and orders from other institutions. A language model fine-tuned on the same data would separate the effect of the decision format from that of domain fine-tuning, and repeated training with different random seeds would quantify the variability of the gains. Methods for constructing answer spaces, by retrieval from curated catalogs or by LLM-generated differentials, should be compared by their inclusion rate and by the downstream accuracy and calibration they yield. Routing should be evaluated on the information available before the read, the order, the indication and the examination type, with institutional worklist assignments as the key. Finally, extending the approach to decisions made on images as well as on text would address the larger part of radiological practice.
